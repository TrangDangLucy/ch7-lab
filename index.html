Section 7.1

Diversification and portfolio risk

One stock carries two kinds of risk. Market risk comes from the whole economy: the business cycle, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates. Unique risk belongs to the firm: its research results, a change of key staff. Holding more stocks cancels more of the unique risk. Market risk hits every stock, so it stays.

Other names you will meet: market risk is also systematic or nondiversifiable risk. Unique risk is also firm-specific, nonsystematic or diversifiable risk.

Watch out

A flat curve does not mean the extra stocks are useless. It means what is left is shared by all stocks.

Diversifying also means dividing a fixed amount of money across assets. Adding fresh money to more assets is a different thing (see 7.5).

Section 7.2

Portfolios of two risky assets

Put a share wD of your money in the bond fund and the rest, wE = 1 − wD, in the stock fund. The portfolio’s expected return is the weighted average of the two expected returns. Its risk is not the weighted average of the two σ’s, because the way the funds move together matters. That is the whole idea of diversification, and the three figures below show it from three angles.

How to read it · Figure 7.3
Axes

Horizontal: the weight in stocks, w(stocks). A second scale underneath shows the weight in bonds, w(bonds) = 1 − w(stocks), running the other way. Vertical: expected return.

The line

It is straight because expected return is a weighted average. It starts at 8% for all bonds (w = 0) and ends at 13% for all stocks (w = 1).

Outside 0 to 1 you are selling one fund short to buy more of the other. With w(D) = 2 and w(E) = −1 the expected return is 2×8 + (−1)×13 = 3%.

Watch out

Correlation does not appear anywhere in this figure. It changes risk, never expected return. That is why the next figure matters.

How to read it · Figure 7.4 (the confusing one)
Axes

Same horizontal axis as Figure 7.3 (weight in stocks). Vertical: portfolio standard deviation σ.

Every curve passes through the same two points: σ = 12% at w = 0 and σ = 20% at w = 1. A single fund’s risk does not depend on correlation, so the curves only differ in between.

The curves

ρ = +1: a straight line between 12% and 20%. Risk is just the weighted average, so there is no diversification benefit.

ρ = 0.30: the curve dips below 12% before climbing. The bottom, at about 18% in stocks, is the minimum-variance portfolio, with σ = 11.45%. That is lower than either fund alone.

ρ = 0: a deeper dip (10.29%). ρ = −1: a V that touches zero at w(E) = 0.375, a perfect hedge.

Watch out

The bottom of the curve is the least risky mix. It is not the best mix, because it also gives up expected return.

The dip below 12% only appears when ρ is below σD/σE = 0.6 (footnote 2 of the chapter). Try ρ = 0.8 with short sales off and the minimum jumps to “all bonds”.

How to read it · Figure 7.5
Axes

Now the horizontal axis is risk (σ) and the vertical axis is expected return. The weight axis is gone. Each point on the curve is one choice of w: read its return from Figure 7.3 and its σ from Figure 7.4, and plot the pair.

The curve

The curve is the portfolio opportunity set: every risk and return pair you can reach by mixing the two funds. The two ends are D (all bonds) and E (all stocks). The leftmost point is the minimum-variance portfolio.

Above that point the curve is efficient (green): for each risk level it gives the most return. Below it, there is always a point directly above with the same risk and more return, so the lower part is inefficient.

Lower ρ pushes the curve left, toward the northwest. At ρ = +1 it collapses to the straight line D to E.

Watch out

Figure 7.5 does not tell you which efficient point to pick. That depends on how much risk you can stomach, which is the topic of 7.3.

Readers often think the curve shows time. It does not. Moving along it only changes the weights.

Section 7.3

Asset allocation with stocks, bonds and bills

Now add a risk-free asset, T-bills yielding rf. Mixing T-bills with any risky portfolio traces a straight line, the capital allocation line (CAL). Its slope is the Sharpe ratio: extra return per unit of risk, (E(r) − rf) / σ. A steeper CAL pays more for each unit of risk, so the best risky portfolio is the one with the steepest CAL. It is called P.

The same as Figure 7.5: risk on the horizontal axis, expected return on the vertical axis. The new point is rf on the vertical axis, where risk is zero.

The lines

Draw a line from rf through any risky portfolio. That is a CAL, and every point on it is a mix of T-bills and that portfolio. Figure 7.6 compares two: through portfolio A (82% bonds, 18% stocks, Sharpe 0.34) and through B (70% bonds, 30% stocks, Sharpe 0.38). B has the steeper line, so B beats A at every level of risk.

Keep rotating the line upward until it just touches the curve. The touching point is P: 40% bonds, 60% stocks, E(r) = 11%, σ = 14.2%, Sharpe 0.42.

Watch out

P is neither the minimum-variance portfolio nor the highest-return portfolio. It is the point with the steepest line from rf.

Try raising rf in the lab. P moves, because the tangent line pivots around a different starting point.

How to read it · Figures 7.8 and 7.9
Axes

Still risk against expected return. The new curve is an indifference curve: every point on it gives the investor the same satisfaction, U = E(r) − ½Aσ².

The picture

The indifference curve bends upward, because taking on more risk needs more return to stay equally happy. The investor wants the highest curve that still touches the CAL. The touching point is C, the optimal complete portfolio.

The pie in Figure 7.9 splits C into T-bills, bonds and stocks. For A = 4: 25.61% bills, 29.76% bonds, 44.63% stocks.

Watch out

Change A and watch the lab. C slides along the CAL, but P does not move. This is the separation property: everyone with the same inputs holds the same risky portfolio P. Only the split between P and T-bills depends on risk aversion.

If y is above 100%, the investor borrows at rf to buy more of P.

Section 7.4

The Markowitz portfolio optimization model

With many risky assets the problem has the same three parts as before. First, map the risk and return combinations you can build. Second, find the risky portfolio with the steepest CAL. Third, let each investor mix it with T-bills. The map in the first part is the minimum-variance frontier: for every target expected return, the lowest σ you can reach. Its upper half, from the global minimum-variance portfolio upward, is the efficient frontier.

How to read it · Figures 7.10 to 7.13
Figure 7.10 · the frontier

Each dot is one asset. The curve on the left edge is the minimum-variance frontier. Every single asset sits to the right, inside it, which is the lesson: holding one asset alone is inefficient, because a mix gets more return for less risk.

The dashed lower half is inefficient. Only the upper half, starting at the global minimum-variance portfolio, is the efficient frontier.

Figure 7.12 · two ways to build it

Method one (squares): fix a required return, draw a horizontal line, and take the leftmost point on it, the smallest σ. Your slider in the lab does exactly this. Method two (circles): fix a σ, draw a vertical line, and take the highest point on it.

Both methods trace the same frontier. Every point comes with its weights, expected return and σ.

Figures 7.11 and 7.13 · the CAL

From rf draw lines to different points on the efficient frontier, such as A, G (the global minimum) and P. Each steeper line beats the one below it. The line that just touches the frontier is the CAL for P, and it dominates every other feasible line.

All clients with the same inputs get the same P (the separation property).

Watch out

The picture is only as good as the input list: garbage in, garbage out. Using last few years’ average returns as forecasts is especially noisy: with σ = 30% and ten years of data, the estimate of the mean has a standard error of 30/√10 = 9.5%.

Each extra constraint (no short sales, a minimum dividend yield, SRI or ESG screens) moves the frontier inward and lowers the Sharpe ratio. Clients should know that cost.

Lab: how many estimates does an analyst need to supply?

The covariance matrix has n×n cells, but it is symmetric, so there are only n(n−1)/2 different covariances. For 50 securities that is 1,225, which is the chapter’s “daunting task”.

Asset allocation and security selection

The math is the same for both: build an efficient frontier and pick a point on it. The investment industry still separates them for organizational reasons. A large firm invests across several markets and asset classes that each need specialists, so each class is optimized on its own, while top management adjusts how much money each class receives.

Section 7.5

Risk pooling, risk sharing and time diversification

Many people argue that stocks become safe over long horizons: any single year can lose, but the market’s positive risk premium should win over decades, so long-horizon investors can hold more stocks. The chapter says this is a misreading of how diversification works. To see why, start with an insurance company.

Lab A: pooling versus sharing

An insurer sells n identical fire policies. Each policy’s payout has a standard deviation of $1,000 and the policies are uncorrelated.

How to read it · pooling and sharing
Pooling

Adding uncorrelated policies makes the average payout more predictable (σ/√n). But the total payout becomes more uncertain, because its standard deviation grows with √n. Pooling alone does not make the owner safer.

Sharing

If the company sells shares so that n investors each own 1/n of the pool, each investor holds the average payout, and the risk per investor shrinks. Pooling plus sharing is the full business model of insurance.

Watch out

Real diversification keeps your budget fixed and splits it up. Putting a second $100,000 into Amazon next to $100,000 in Microsoft adds risk. Splitting the first $100,000 across both reduces it.

The pro-horizon argument

The standard deviation of the average yearly return falls as 30%/√T, while its mean stays at 5%. So the chance that the average is positive climbs toward 100%. The left chart shows that rise: 56.6% after 1 year, 81.9% after 30.

The missing half

Your wealth depends on the total return, and its standard deviation grows as 30% × √T. So the bad outcomes get worse. The right chart shows the loss in the worst 1% of cases: 47.7% of your money after 1 year, 90.2% after 30.

Watch out

A higher probability of gain is not the same as a safer investment. Stretching the horizon is like adding policies without adding shareholders. True time diversification would mean putting only half as much in stocks each year for a 2-year plan, which halves the exposure to each year.

Equations and terms

Key equations

Eq. 7.2E(rp) = wDE(rD) + wEE(rE)

Expected return is a weighted average. Correlation does not enter.

Eq. 7.3σp² = wD²σD² + wE²σE² + 2wDwECov(rD,rE)

Variance is not a weighted average. The covariance term is where diversification happens.

Eq. 7.6Cov(rD,rE) = ρDEσDσE

Example: 0.30 × 12 × 20 = 72.

Eq. 7.9 (ρ = +1 only)σp = wDσD + wEσE

The only case where portfolio risk is the weighted average of the two risks.

Eq. 7.12 (ρ = −1)wD = σE / (σD + σE)

The perfect hedge: 20/32 = 0.625 in bonds gives zero risk.

Footnote 3wMin(D) = (σE² − Cov) / (σD² + σE² − 2Cov)

Minimum-variance weight. Example: (400 − 72)/(144 + 400 − 144) = 0.82.

Sharpe ratioSp = [E(rp) − rf] / σp

Slope of the CAL. Higher is better.

Eq. 7.13wD = [E(RD)σE² − E(RE)Cov] / [E(RD)σE² + E(RE)σD² − (E(RD) + E(RE))Cov]

Optimal risky portfolio P. R means excess return over rf.

Eq. 7.14y = [E(rP) − rf] / (AσP²)

Share of wealth in P. Use decimals for returns and σ.

Eq. 7.15 and 7.16σp² = ΣiΣj wiwjCov(ri,rj)

Portfolio variance for any number of assets, from the bordered covariance matrix.

Eq. 7.20σp² = (1/n)σ̄² + ((n−1)/n)Cov̄

Equal weights: average variance fades with n, average covariance stays.

Eq. 7.21σp² = (1/n)σ² + ((n−1)/n)ρσ²

Same σ and ρ for all stocks. As n grows the variance approaches ρσ².

Eq. 7.22 and 7.23Var(Σxi) = nσ² and Var(Σxi/n) = σ²/n

Total payout gets riskier with n. Average payout gets safer.

Time horizon TSD(total) = σ√T and SD(average) = σ/√T

Why longer horizons raise total risk while average risk falls.

Key terms in plain words

Diversification
Splitting a fixed amount of money across many assets so no single source of risk dominates.
Insurance principle
Risk falls when many independent risks are spread out, as an insurer does with many policies.
Market, systematic, nondiversifiable risk
Risk from economy-wide factors that hit nearly every security. It remains after diversifying.
Unique, firm-specific, nonsystematic, diversifiable risk
Risk that belongs to one company and cancels out across many.
Short sale
Selling an asset you do not own. It gives a negative portfolio weight.
Portfolio opportunity set
All risk and return pairs you can reach by mixing the available assets.
Minimum-variance portfolio
The mix with the smallest possible standard deviation.
Minimum-variance frontier
For each target return, the lowest-variance portfolio, plotted as a curve.
Efficient frontier of risky assets
The upper part of the minimum-variance frontier, from the global minimum upward.
Input list
The expected returns, variances and covariances fed into the optimizer.
Capital allocation line (CAL)
All combinations of T-bills and one risky portfolio. Its slope is the Sharpe ratio.
Sharpe ratio
Extra return over the risk-free rate, per unit of standard deviation.
Optimal risky portfolio (P)
The risky mix with the highest Sharpe ratio, where the CAL touches the frontier.
Risk aversion (A)
How much return an investor demands to accept variance. Utility is U = E(r) − ½Aσ².
Indifference curve
Risk and return pairs that give the investor equal satisfaction.
Separation property
Step one, finding P, is technical and the same for everyone. Step two, how much to hold in P, depends on risk aversion.
Risk pooling
Combining many independent risks in one pot.
Risk sharing
Splitting the pooled risk among many owners so each keeps a small slice.

What to remember

  • A portfolio’s expected return is a weighted average. Its risk is not.
  • Unless two assets are perfectly correlated, combining them gives less risk than the weighted average of their risks. The lower the correlation, the bigger the gain.
  • An asset with negative correlation to the rest works as a hedge, and at ρ = −1 risk can reach zero.
  • The efficient frontier gives the highest expected return for each level of risk. Rational investors choose on it.
  • Different managers build different frontiers because their forecasts differ. Poor analysis can make an active portfolio worse than a plain index fund.
  • With a risk-free asset and identical inputs, every investor holds the same risky portfolio P and differs only in how much goes to T-bills.
  • True diversification divides a fixed budget. Putting more money at risk does not reduce dollar risk.
  • A longer horizon behaves like adding more bets. Total risk rises, so “time diversification” is an illusion.
  • Insurance works through pooling and sharing together.
